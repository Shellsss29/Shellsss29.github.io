Why Apple Photos Thinks You're 5 Different People (And How to Fix It)

Apple still can't fix this photo problem. Here's what my facial recognition research taught me about solving it.

Shailly Bhati Software Engineer | AI/ML Enthusiast

The Problem

I opened Apple Photos last week and found myself listed as 5 different people. Same face, but the app created separate entries for each one. I merged them manually. Two days later? Split again.

If you use an iPhone, you've probably dealt with this too. The facial recognition just keeps creating duplicates, and it's been like this for years.

Why Does This Happen?

Apple Photos scans your face in each photo and creates a mathematical signature for it. Think of it like a fingerprint, but for your face. Then it groups similar signatures together. Each group becomes a "person" in your People album.

The problem is that your face signature changes based on:

  • Lighting (outdoor photos vs. indoor photos look completely different)
  • Time (you from 5 years ago vs. you today)
  • Angle (front-facing selfie vs. side profile at a party)
  • What you're wearing (glasses, hats, makeup)
  • Photo quality (crisp iPhone 15 photo vs. grainy old picture)

So the system looks at two photos of you and thinks, "These signatures are too different. Must be two different people." And boom, you get duplicates.

Why Apple Hasn't Solved It

Here's the thing: Apple is stuck between two bad options. If they make the grouping too strict, you get what we have now (one person split into 5). But if they make it too loose, your photos of your friend Mike might get mixed with photos of some random guy at a concert.

Apple picked the "safe" choice. They'd rather annoy you with duplicates than accidentally mix up different people's faces. I get it, but it's still frustrating when you have to manually merge the same person every week.

A Better Way: Stop Looking at Just the Face

The fix isn't that complicated. Apple Photos is only looking at your face. But there's so much other information in your photos that could help.

Look at the context too

Your photos have metadata built in. Use it:

  • Location: If two faces keep showing up at your home, they're probably the same person
  • Time: Photos taken within the same hour or day? Likely the same people
  • Clothing: People tend to wear similar styles and colors. That's another signal
  • Body build: Height, posture, body shape don't change like faces do in different lighting

Group smarter, not harder

Instead of trying to cram everything into one perfect group per person, create smaller groups first. Like "you with glasses" and "you without glasses" as separate mini-groups. Then link those mini-groups together based on location, time, and other clues.

This gives you the best of both worlds. Strict grouping where it matters, but smart linking when the context makes it obvious it's the same person.

Be flexible with matching

Not all photos are equal. A blurry photo from 2015 should get more wiggle room than a crystal-clear selfie from yesterday. If the location and time match, lower the strictness. If they don't, keep it tight.

What I Learned Building Face Recognition Systems

During my master's at Northeastern, I spent months working on an Image Spoof Detection project. The goal was to tell real faces apart from fake ones (photos, videos, masks).

The challenge was similar to Apple's problem, just flipped. We needed to catch when someone tried to trick the system with a photo of a face instead of a real person. That meant understanding how faces change across lighting, angles, and quality while still being able to tell real from fake.

The big lesson? You can't just look at the face alone. We had to analyze skin texture (real skin has pores, photos don't), depth information (real faces are 3D, photos are flat), and movement patterns (real people blink and move naturally).

What's interesting is that the same techniques work for Apple's duplicate problem. When we analyzed how faces change under different lighting or angles, we learned which features stay consistent and which ones don't. Those consistent features are exactly what Apple should focus on for clustering.

We also found that looking at multiple signals at once (face + texture + depth + timing) was way more reliable than just looking at the face. That's the same approach that would fix Apple Photos.

What You Can Do Right Now

Apple probably won't fix this anytime soon, so here's how to deal with it:

Merge them manually

  1. Open Photos and go to the People album
  2. Tap "Select" in the top right
  3. Select all the duplicates of the same person
  4. Tap "Merge" at the bottom

It's annoying, but it takes like 30 seconds.

Name everyone

Give your people actual names. I've noticed that named people seem to get split less often. The system might be learning from your corrections when names are attached.

Don't go crazy with filters

Heavy filters and edits can completely change how your face looks to the algorithm. If you want better clustering, keep your photos close to the originals.

What Apple Should Actually Do

If I were on the Photos team, here's what I'd push for:

Quick wins

Show us how confident the system is. Like "85% sure this is the same person." That way we know which merges are safe and which ones need a closer look.

Also, let the system learn from our corrections. If I keep merging the same two clusters, that should teach it something.

The real fix

Use all the data you already have. Location, time, depth sensors from the camera. iPhones collect this stuff anyway. Stop throwing it away and just looking at faces.

And let the system learn from each person's photo library individually instead of using the same generic algorithm for everyone.

Final Thoughts

This isn't some impossible problem. Apple's clustering algorithm is just too simple. It only looks at faces and ignores everything else.

The solution is straightforward: use the context. Location, time, clothing, body build. All that data is already sitting there in your photos. Just use it.

From working on facial recognition and spoof detection, I know this stuff is solvable. The techniques exist. Someone at Apple just needs to prioritize it.

Until then, I guess we're all stuck manually merging duplicates every week.
